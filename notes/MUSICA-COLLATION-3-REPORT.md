## 2. The remaining quotations

### Jowett, *Politics* VIII.5, 1340a–b (c-ethos) — corrected

The copy is Benjamin Jowett’s *Politics* in *The Works of Aristotle*, vol. 10, under W. D. Ross (archive.org, theworksofaristo10arisuoft). Gutenberg eBook 6762 is William Ellis, and it was not used. The djvu text doubles spaces and misreads letters (“arc” for “are,” “sonic” for “some”). Those were repaired from the words around them. The semicolon in “Dorian; the Phrygian” has no space before it. The djvu prints a space there because it spaces every word.

The marginal numbers put “On the other hand” in 1340a and “so-called Mixolydian” at 1340b. The cite is now “Aristotle, *Politics* VIII.5, 1340a–b.” The quotation is his two sentences, with an ellipsis between them. The ellipsis skips “The whole subject has been well treated by philosophical writers on this branch of education, and they confirm their arguments by facts.”

The old second sentence, “The whole of music is of the nature of an imitation, and the different kinds of melody and rhythm are clearly imitations of different kinds of character,” is not in Jowett. It was removed. In its place is his sentence on rhythms: “The same principles apply to rhythms; some have a character of rest, others of motion, and of these latter again, some have a more vulgar, others a nobler movement.”

The study question that asked what follows from the removed sentence now asks what he says of rhythms. The true option, “Some have a character of rest, others of motion,” is not the unique longest. The review of “even in mere melodies” no longer cites the removed sentence. It points to the rhythms sentence, which is now the last sentence of the quotation. “On the other hand, even in mere melodies” is his, so the questions that turn on the word “even” were left.

### Jowett, *Politics* VIII.7, 1341b–1342a (c-ethos) — corrected

The same volume. “We accept” stands in chapter 7, which begins at 1341b. The number 1342a stands just before “by us, but not all of them in the same manner,” and the education sentence follows it. The cite is now “Aristotle, *Politics* VIII.7, 1341b–1342a.”

The old wording, “ethical, practical, and enthusiastic,” “a nature of its own,” and “when others perform,” is not his. The quotation is now: “We accept the division of melodies proposed by certain philosophers into ethical melodies, melodies of action, and passionate or inspiring melodies, each having, as they say, a mode corresponding to it. … In education the most ethical modes are to be preferred, but in listening to the performances of others we may admit the modes of action and passion also.” The ellipsis skips the paragraph on education, purgation, and recreation, and the sentence “It is clear, therefore, that all the modes must be employed by us, but not all of them in the same manner.” No study question quoted the old sentence.

### Ross, *Metaphysics* I.1 (c-musician) — corrected

The copy is the 1908 Oxford translation, as transcribed on Wikisource (*Metaphysics* (Ross, 1908)/Book 1), including the validated page of the 1908 book. The Internet Classics text of the same translator drops “And,” the comma after “arises,” and “But yet,” and it was not followed.

The quotation is now: “And art arises, when from many notions gained by experience one universal judgement about a class of objects is produced. … But yet we think that knowledge and understanding belong to art rather than to experience, and we suppose artists to be wiser than men of experience … and this because the former know the cause, but the latter do not.” The first ellipsis skips the example of Callias and Socrates. The second skips “(which implies that Wisdom depends in all cases rather on knowledge);”. Ross’s spelling “judgement” is kept. His italics on “knowledge” and “understanding” were not added. The cite remains *Metaphysics* I.1. No study question quoted the shortened form.

### Ross, *Metaphysics* VII.17 (c-intact) — corrected

Wikisource’s transcription of the 1908 Book 7 stops in chapter 4. The later page images are not on that site. The sentences below are the Internet Classics text of Ross, which is the same translation, and the dash characters in its long syllable sentence are printed there as hyphens (“syllable-now,” “something-not,” “else:-if”). Those places were not quoted, because the 1908 punctuation was not seen.

The first sentence is quoted whole: “Therefore what we seek is the cause, i.e. the form, by reason of which the matter is some definite thing; and this is the substance of the thing.” The course had dropped “Therefore,” written “that is” for “i.e.,” and begun a new sentence at “This is the substance.”

What follows is two clauses that have no hyphen of that kind, with an ellipsis where the argument between them is skipped: “The syllable is not its elements, ba is not the same as b and a, nor is flesh fire and earth. … But it would seem that this ‘other’ is something, and not an element, and that it is the cause which makes this thing flesh and that a syllable.” The single quotes around “other” are curly, as elsewhere in these files. The Classics text has straight quotes. “Its letters” and “the cause of their being one” are not Ross, and they are no longer inside the quotation. The cite remains *Metaphysics* VII.17. No study question quoted the old second sentence.

### *De anima* (c-soul) — corrected

The copy is J. A. Smith’s translation, the Oxford translation as carried by the Internet Classics Archive. The old quotation joined II.1 and II.2 under the cite “De anima II.1,” and the words “first actuality,” “something of a body,” and “for this reason” are not his. Smith says “the first grade of actuality.” The file prints “csnnot he” where the sentence reads “cannot be.” Other copies of this translation have “cannot be,” and that is what is quoted.

The two sentences are now separate cites. II.1, 412a27: “That is why the soul is the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it.” II.2, 414a19–22: “Hence the rightness of the view that the soul cannot be without a body, while it cannot be a body; it is not a body but something relative to a body. That is why it is in a body, and a body of a definite kind.”

I.4 was also a condensation. The sentence “Rather the soul is that which causes the mixture, as being the cause of the body” is not in Smith. The quotation is now his refutation, cited I.4, 407b–408a: the supporters’ claim that the soul is a kind of harmony, then “Harmony, however, is a certain proportion or composition of the constituents blended, and soul can be neither the one nor the other of these,” then, after an ellipsis that skips the paragraph on movement and on health, “That soul is a harmony in the sense of the mode of composition of the parts of the body is a view easily refutable.” The return no longer says that Aristotle calls the soul the cause of the mixture. It says what this quotation says, and it keeps St. Thomas’s teaching that the soul is the form of the body. The study review that had said “the soul is not the mixture but its cause” now follows the quotation. Smith is named only in the Sources sentence.

### Johannes de Grocheio (c-city) — paraphrased

Constant J. Mews, John N. Crossley, Catherine Jeffreys, Leigh McKinnon, and Carol J. Williams, *Ars musice* (2011), was not opened. The Internet Archive copy is restricted, and the Western Michigan PDF returned 403. There are no quotation marks.

The lead was tightened to the Latin of the epic song, the sentence beginning “Cantus autem iste debet antiquis et civibus laborantibus et mediocribus ministrari.” The course had dropped those of middle station (*mediocribus*) and had said that they “return to their work,” which is stronger than “alacrius aggrediatur,” that each takes up his own work more readily. It now says that the epic song is offered to the old, to working citizens, and to those of middle station, while they rest from their usual work, so that, hearing the hardships and calamities of others, they bear their own more easily and each takes up his own work more readily, and that this serves the preservation of the whole city. “He calls the music of ordinary life the music of the city” was also loose. The lead now says that he orders the music of ordinary life by the practice of the city, which is the plan of the treatise and not a title he gives. The study question says “take up their own work,” and the wrong answer that had said “the music of the city” now uses the same words as the lead. The true option was not changed, and it is not the unique longest.

### Peter of Auvergne, on *Politics* VIII, lect. 2 (c-city) — matched, as a rendering of the Latin

Lidia Lanza’s 2021 edition, and a page image of the Parma edition, vol. 21, were not reached. The Latin was read on corpusthomisticum.org, *In Politicorum continuatio*, “Textum Taurini 1951,” book 8, lectio 2, which is the printed text of this continuation.

“Non enim quantumcumque communicandum est ei; sed inquantum utilis est ad rempublicam” has *musica* as the antecedent of *ei*, from the sentence before it (“communicare musicam”). The course’s “For music is not to be shared in to any extent whatever, but so far as it is useful to the commonwealth” follows that. The next clauses, on melodies, rhythms, and instruments, match “et considerando qualibus melodiis et qualibus rhythmis communicandum est. Adhuc etiam in qualibus organis erudiendum et exercitandum.” The ellipsis then skips the sentences on good and bad melodies, through “Et per hoc tolletur praedicta increpatio,” to “Omnia enim quae circa iuniores sunt, oportet commensurari et determinari secundum hoc quod utilia sunt ad finem reipublicae, quod est bonum perfectum secundum intellectum.” The second English sentence is that one. The cite already says “rendered from the Latin,” and the quotation marks were kept because the Latin was checked. No translator was invented, and none was added to the Sources lesson.
