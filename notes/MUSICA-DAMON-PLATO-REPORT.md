Bodin's "harmonic justice" is measured first by the two justices. Distributive justice follows geometrical proportion, and commutative justice the arithmetical mean (II–II q.61 a.2). His harmonic proportion is a blend of those two, and St. Thomas names no third justice beside them. What remains, the regard for the circumstances of a case, is assigned to equity (II–II q.120 a.1) as the second point. The same order is in the lead, in the third return, and in the study question. The Knolles quotation is unchanged.

Damon and the *Laws* are argued in "Music and the city," and named briefly in c-ethos. Damon's own writings are said to be lost. The *Laws* run from the training of pleasure and pain, through the decline into a rule of the spectators, to songs fixed by law. St. Thomas receives the claim that music shapes character and the city (II–II q.91 a.2, and Peter of Auvergne on *Politics* VIII). He does not receive the fixing of every song (I–II q.96 a.2–3). The end of the music is God's praise and the right ordering of the soul, not the city alone (II–II q.91 a.1–2). That judgment is stated, and it is not offered as a proof from the ratios.

c-city has four new study questions, fourteen in all. The true option is the unique longest in 4 of the 14, which is within one third. c-ethos was not given new questions. The ten questions there are still true of that page.

### Paraphrased, and checked on the page in this pass

These are not in quotation marks in the course.

- *Republic* IV, 424b, Shorey, same text. "For a change to a new type of music is something to beware of as a hazard of all our fortunes." The course says this in its own sentence and keeps "a hazard of all our fortunes."
- *Republic* III, 398c–400c, Shorey, same text, sections 398c, 399e, 400a, 400b, and 400c. Song and tune are taken up at 398c. At 399e the rhythms are those "of a life that is orderly and brave." At 400a the foot and the air are to conform to the speech, and not the speech to the foot and the tune. At 400b they will take counsel with Damon as to which feet suit illiberality, insolence, or madness, and which rhythms are to be kept for the opposite character. At 400c the matter is postponed for Damon's consideration. The course paraphrases this. It does not quote it. Jowett was not read for these lines. The Jowett block already on c-ethos (398d–399c) was not changed and was not rechecked.
- *Laws* II, 653a–660a, Bury, same Laws text. Education is the right training of pleasure and pain, so as to hate what ought to be hated, before a rational account (653a–b). Choir-training takes in dancing and song, and the educated man is fully choir-trained (654a–b). Where the spectators award the prize, the poets write to please them (659–660). The three named choruses, of the Muses, Apollo, and Dionysus, are at 664, outside this range, and they are not claimed.
- *Laws* II, 657a, Bury. Tunes that have a natural correctness can be enacted by law and consecrated. Paraphrased as: tunes that are naturally right can be fixed by law and consecrated. The words "permanently consecrated" are not quoted.
- *Laws* III, 700a–701b, Bury. The kinds of song had been fixed, and it was forbidden to set one kind of words to another kind of tune. Later the poets mixed every kind, and the pleasure of the hearer became the standard. After the sentence quoted at 701a, the steps run from conceit of wisdom and contempt for law, to the refusal to obey rulers, then parents, then the laws, and at last the loss of respect for oaths, pledges, or divinities (701b). The course says "oaths and for the gods." It does not quote "Titans."
- *Laws* VII, 799a–802e, Bury. The device is the consecrating of all dancing and all music, each hymn to its god (799a). At 800a no one may sing or dance against the public songs any more than against any other law. That sentence was not quoted, because Bury's wording turns on "nome." The lawgiver's outline of the songs (802) is inside the citation and is not set out by itself.
- *Summa theologiae* II–II q.91 a.1, Dominican Province translation (1920), newadvent.org/summa/3091.htm, read in this pass. "not indeed for His sake, but for our own sake; since by praising Him our devotion is aroused towards Him" and "ascends in his affections to God." The course paraphrases this, with American "toward," and does not quote it. Article 2 was already quoted on c-ethos. The new sentences paraphrase the faint-hearted and the movement of the soul.
