## 2. Jowett's Plato

The copy read was Benjamin Jowett, *The Republic*, third edition, Project Gutenberg eBook 1497, and the same edition in the Archive.org text of volume 3 of *The Dialogues of Plato* (Jowett-3rded-v3.txt). Gutenberg prints the sentences continuously. The Archive text confirms the same wording where a line break does not split a word. Neither copy prints a clean marginal Stephanus letter beside these sentences. The Archive text does place the number 445 at "Still our old question," which is the question after the sentence on virtue, so 444d–e is the right number for what precedes it.

A phrase below in quotation marks was copied from the page and found, as that string, in Gutenberg. An ellipsis in the course is a skip. The skipped words are named.

### *Republic* VII, 531c (c-please)

The old sentence was not Jowett's. It began "They are like astronomers; they are in quest of the numbers which are to be found in these heard concords, but they never attain to the natural harmonies of number." "Heard concords" does not occur in this translation. The citation was kept, and the English is now his sentence: "For they too are in error, like the astronomers; they investigate the numbers of the harmonies which are heard, but they never attain to problems—that is to say, they never reach the natural harmonies of number, or reflect why some numbers are harmonious and others not." The em dash in "problems—that" is his. The sentence is the one that stands at 531c in the received division. The next sentences in Jowett are the reply that this is more than mortal knowledge, and then the kinship of the studies (531c–d).

The same verb was put back wherever a lesson had quoted a shortened form of this sentence and given it Shorey's word "ascend."

- lib-2 now quotes "they never attain to problems". It had said, in italics, "they do not ascend to problems".
- end-1 now quotes "they investigate the numbers of the harmonies which are heard, but they never attain to problems." It had said, in italics and with a capital, "They seek the numbers in these heard concords, and do not ascend to problems."
- The sources line of end-1 now says "who do not attain to problems". That line is a label, not a quotation.
- ex-means says "never attain to problems", not in quotation marks. It had said "never ascend to problems".
- The study question on lib-2 has the true option "and never attain to problems". "Attain" is the same length as "ascend," so the lengths of the options did not change. A later study item still says "stopping short of problems," and that item is a paraphrase.

### *Republic* IV, 443c–e (c-virtue)

The whole sentence matches Gutenberg, including "concernment of man" and the British spelling "co-operates." Two dashes had spaces that Jowett does not print. "others, — he" is now "others,—he", and "intervals — when" is now "intervals—when". Both dashes are em dashes, with no space on either side. The citation was kept.

### *Republic* IV, 444d–e (c-virtue)

Two sentences in the old quotation are not in Jowett, here or elsewhere in the dialogue: "And is not good habit also a true order, and bad habit a want of order, in the body?" and "Then virtue is the order of the soul, as health is the order of the body." "Order of the soul" does not occur. "Good habit" occurs in Jowett's introduction, not in this speech. Those sentences were removed. They were not rewritten as a paraphrase, because the questions he does print already say that virtue is the health of the soul and that justice is a natural order in its parts.

The quotation is now those questions, with the replies "True." and "Exactly so, he said." left out under ellipsis: "And the creation of health is the institution of a natural order and government of one by another in the parts of the body; and the creation of disease is the production of a state of things at variance with this natural order? … And is not the creation of justice the institution of a natural order and government of one by another in the parts of the soul, and the creation of injustice the production of a state of things at variance with the natural order? … Then virtue is the health and beauty and well-being of the soul, and vice the disease and weakness and deformity of the same?" Each piece matches Gutenberg, and the Archive text has the same sentences. The citation 444d–e was kept.

### *Republic* III, 398d–399c (c-ethos)

The old quotation could not be matched. "The Mixolydian, he replied, and the tense Lydian," "the soft and convivial," "which are called relaxed," and "Then, I said, Dorian and Phrygian remain" are not Jowett's words. "Mixolydian" and "tense Lydian" do not occur in this translation.

The quotation is now his wording. The harmonies of sorrow are "the mixed or tenor Lydian, and the full-toned or bass Lydian, and such like," and they "must be banished." The ellipsis then skips the lines on drunkenness, softness, and the guardians. The soft or drinking harmonies are "The Ionian, he replied, and the Lydian; they are termed 'relaxed.'" The single quotation marks are his. Glaucon, not Socrates, answers the question about military use: "and if so the Dorian and the Phrygian are the only ones which you have left." Both pieces match Gutenberg, and the Archive text has "mixed or tenor Lydian."

In the received division the question about sorrow is 398e and Glaucon's reply is 399a. The citation 398d–399c names the discussion of which harmonies to keep, and it was left. The study questions that mention the Mixolydian cite Aristotle's *Politics*, which still says "Mixolydian," and they were not changed.

## 3. Equity, II–II q.120 a.1

The article was read in the Latin on corpusthomisticum.org (the text of IIª-IIae qq. 109–120; the separate page for q.120 is not served) and in the Dominican translation on newadvent.org/summa/3120.htm.

The corpus says that in some cases to follow the enacted law is against the equality of justice and against the common good (*contra aequalitatem iustitiae, et contra bonum commune*); that it is good, the words of the law set aside, to follow what the account of justice and the common utility require (*praetermissis verbis legis, sequi id quod poscit iustitiae ratio et communis utilitas*); and that equity (*epieikeia*, *aequitas*) is ordered to this. The reply to the second objection says that this is a judgment on a particular case that has arisen (*de aliquo particulari negotio*), not a judgment on the law. The reply to the third says that interpretation belongs to doubtful cases and needs the ruler's determination, and that a manifest case needs execution, not interpretation.

The Dominican translation says: "if the law be applied to certain cases it will frustrate the equality of justice and be injurious to the common good"; "it is good to set aside the letter of the law and to follow the dictates of justice and the common good"; and, in the reply to the second objection, "some particular case." The course follows that English. It says "what justice and the common good require," which is the Dominican phrase, and "a particular case," which is the reply to the second objection.

Three claims on the page did not match the article. Equity was said only to set the letter aside "where following it would injure the common good," with no mention of the equality of justice. The remainder of Bodin's claim was called "the regard for the particular circumstances," which is Bodin's phrase and not the article's. That remainder was also assigned to the prudence of the ruler, in the lead, and to the prudence of the judge, in the third return, under the citation of q.120 a.1. The article does not make equity the prudence of the judge.

The lead, the third return, and the study review now say the same thing: in a particular case where following the law would frustrate the equality of justice and be injurious to the common good, equity sets aside the letter of the law and follows what justice and the common good require. The order is still the two justices (II–II q.61 a.2), and then equity. The true option now reads "and only then assigns the particular case to equity". It is not the unique longest option. c-city has 14 questions, and the true option is the unique longest in 4 of them. The honesty line, "justice and equity at II–II q.61 and q.120," was left.
