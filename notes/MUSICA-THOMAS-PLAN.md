## Sources checked

Every text below was read in the place named before it is quoted. English of the *Summa* is the Dominican Province translation (newadvent.org, 2nd ed. 1920); Latin is from corpusthomisticum.org. Texts with no free English (the *De regno*, the commentaries) are rendered from the Latin, as the course already does for Boethius and Augustine.

- *ST* II–II q.91 a.1 (praise with the lips arouses devotion in ourselves and our hearers) and a.2 entire: the body (melodies dispose the soul; Augustine, *Confessions* X.33 and IX.6); ad 1 (spiritual canticles may be sung outwardly); ad 2 (Jerome blames singing for display or pleasure, not singing; Augustine on being moved more by the voice than by the words); ad 3 (preaching is the nobler way, so deacons and prelates should not be taken up with singing); ad 4 (instruments, from *Politics* VIII: they move to pleasure rather than form a good disposition; their use in the Old Law); ad 5 (singing for pleasure distracts, singing for devotion attends; Augustine’s “hidden correspondence”).
- *ST* I–II q.22 a.3 (passion is properly in the sensitive appetite, with bodily change); q.24 a.1 (passions are morally good or evil as subject to reason and will), a.3 (it belongs to the perfection of moral good that a man be moved to good in his sensitive appetite as well as his will; passion that follows reason increases the good of an act); q.25 a.2 (love is the first of the passions).
- *ST* I–II q.31 a.6 (the senses are loved for knowledge and for use; delight for the sake of knowledge is proper to man); q.32 a.1 ad 3 (operations please when proportionate to the agent; so leisure and play please); q.32 a.7 (likeness causes pleasure; measure in bodily goods).
- *ST* I–II q.55 (virtue a good operative habit); q.59 a.5 and ad 1 (moral virtue is not without passion: “Virtue overcomes inordinate passion; it produces ordinate passion”); q.64 a.1–2 (the mean is conformity to the rule of reason; the mean that reason puts into the matter).
- *ST* II–II q.168 a.2 (a virtue about play, *eutrapelia*; the sed contra quotes Augustine’s *De musica* II; three cautions; Ambrose on “harmony which is the concord of good works”), a.3 (excess in play), a.4 (lack of mirth is a vice).
- *ST* II–II q.141 a.4 ad 3 (pleasure in “a well-harmonized sound” is for its becomingness and has nothing to do with the preservation of nature); q.180 a.2 ad 3 (beauty is found radically in reason).
- *ST* I q.81 a.3 ad 2 (reason rules the irascible and concupiscible “by a politic power,” because the sensitive appetite has something of its own); I q.84 a.6 (sense knowledge is not the total cause of intellectual knowledge but “in a way the material cause”; the agent intellect abstracts).
- *ST* I–II q.90 a.2 (law is directed to the common good); q.95 a.1 (law trains to virtue, chiefly by withdrawing men from undue pleasures, “especially the young”); II–II q.61 a.2 (distributive justice follows geometrical proportion, commutative arithmetical); II–II q.120 a.1 (equity, *epikeia*, applies justice where the letter of the law fails).
- *De regno* (in the numbering of the Corpus Thomisticum) I.16 (the king’s office orders all particular goods to the good of the multitude) and II.4 (a pleasant site for a city, used moderately; superfluous pleasures dull the senses, corrupt the judgment of prudence, and make men soft; “a little pleasure, as a seasoning, so that men’s minds may be refreshed”).
- *Sententia libri Politicorum* I lect. 3 n.9 (St. Thomas’s own part: reason rules appetite by a political and royal rule).
- The commentary on the *Politics*, Book VIII. St. Thomas’s own commentary ends at III.6; the rest, including Book VIII, is by Peter of Auvergne (Petrus de Alvernia), master of arts at Paris, who completed it after St. Thomas’s death. Read in the Parma edition, vol. 21 (1867), Book VIII lect. 2–3, which is the text printed with St. Thomas’s works. Points used: music is sought for play and rest, for virtue, and for intellectual delight; hearing consists in a certain mean ratio, so proportioned sound delights it; reason in hearing judges the ratio of tones, and the delight of that judgment is proportioned to the delight in moral works that stand in the same proportion, so exercise in judging and delighting rightly is an exercise toward virtue; everything taught the young is to be measured by what serves the end of the commonwealth; instruments that excite the spirits immoderately (the *fistula*, the aulos) are for other occasions and not for education; some melodies purge harmful passions. The Parma text is an OCR scan, so it is paraphrased, not quoted, except for a phrase or two read clearly.
- *Super Boetium De Trinitate* q.5 a.3 ad 6 (music a middle science: it considers sounds “not as they are sounds, but as they are proportionable according to numbers”); q.5 a.1 ad 3 (already used).

## Later voices (2b)

- Jean Bodin, *The Six Bookes of a Commonweale* VI.6, tr. Richard Knolles (London, 1606), pp. 755 and 760–61: royal monarchy governed by “an Harmonicall mixture of Iustice, composed of Iustice Distributiue, or Geometricall; and Commutatiue, or Arithmeticall”; the law, equity, the execution of the law, and the office of the magistrate as the numbers 4, 6, 8, 12, whose transposition destroys “the Harmonie of the Commonweale, and musicall consent thereof.” Checked in the archive.org scan of the 1606 edition (OCR read against the sense).
- Johannes Kepler, *Harmonices mundi* (Linz, 1619) III, the *Digressio politica* (pp. 86 ff.): Kepler sets out Bodin’s chapter to bring light to a place obscure in itself, because Bodin “did not bring enough help from the mathematical disciplines”; he compares the popular state to arithmetic, aristocracy to geometric, and kingship to harmonic proportion, and corrects Bodin’s definition of the harmonic mean. Checked in the archive.org scan of the 1619 Latin; paraphrased. The Aiton, Duncan, and Field translation was not available to me.
- Johannes de Grocheio, *Ars musice* (Paris, about 1300): music of the city (*musica civilis* or *vulgaris*), the epic song sung to the old and to working citizens at rest, so that hearing of others’ hardships they bear their own and return to work more readily, for the preservation of the whole city; the crowned song sung before kings and nobles; dances that turn the young from wrong thoughts. Neither the Rohloff nor the Mews edition was available, so this is paraphrased from general knowledge and listed as unverified.

## The Thomistic account (c-ethos)

The lead of c-ethos (the section on character) keeps the governing sentence and adds a connected account in five steps, written as an argument:

1. The fact: melodies dispose the soul in different ways (II–II q.91 a.2, from Aristotle and Boethius).
2. Where the fact lands: passion is a movement of the sensitive appetite (I–II q.22 a.3), and the sensitive appetite “has something of its own,” so reason rules it by a political and not a despotic power (I q.81 a.3 ad 2).
3. Why that matters for virtue: it belongs to the perfection of moral good that a man be moved to the good in his sensitive appetite as well as in his will (I–II q.24 a.3); virtue “produces ordinate passion” (q.59 a.5 ad 1); and the mean of virtue is the mean that reason puts into the passions (q.64 a.1–2).
4. What delight adds: delight in a well-proportioned sound is proper to man and is for its becomingness (I–II q.31 a.6; II–II q.141 a.4 ad 3); Peter of Auvergne, completing St. Thomas’s commentary on *Politics* VIII, says that judging the ratios of tones and delighting in them rightly is an exercise toward virtue.
5. Who judges the use: prudence, under charity in worship (II–II q.91 a.2 ad 2, ad 4, ad 5), and fitting recreation is itself a virtue (II–II q.168 a.2).

Then the principle: music states this account from moral philosophy and theology; it does not demonstrate it, and the ratios of a string are not its premises (q.5 a.3 ad 6: music considers sounds as proportionable by number).

## “Music and the City” (c-city)

A new contemplation, C7, after c-ethos; the later ones move down one number. Written as an argument in the lead:

1. What music offers the city: the fact of c-ethos (melodies dispose the soul) and its consequence in common life; Aristotle’s and Plato’s laws recalled, not repeated; Grocheio’s music of the city as one witness of the use.
2. What the city may ask of music: law is directed to the common good (I–II q.90 a.2) and trains to virtue, especially the young (q.95 a.1); Peter of Auvergne: what is taught the young is measured by the end of the commonwealth, and instruments that excite immoderately are kept out of education; the ruler provides moderate pleasure as a seasoning, so that minds are refreshed, and guards against superfluous pleasures that corrupt the judgment of prudence (*De regno* II.4); fitting recreation is a virtue (II–II q.168 a.2–4).
3. How St. Thomas judges both: music offers a real but disposing power; the city may order its use, but only to the common good, and the judgment is of prudence, not of harmonics. Bodin’s harmonic justice and Kepler’s correction of it are measured by II–II q.61 a.2 (two proportions in justice, geometrical and arithmetical) and q.120 a.1 (regard for particular circumstances belongs to equity): the musical figure names a real need in a commonwealth, but the proportion is a figure for prudence and equity, not a third kind of justice demonstrated from ratios.
4. The principle: music states these applications to politics, and their demonstration belongs to political philosophy.

First encounter: *De regno* II.4 (Latin and English). Returns: (1) Peter of Auvergne on Politics VIII (rendered), recast on the end of the commonwealth; (2) II–II q.168 a.2 (Dominican tr.), recast on recreation as a virtue; (3) Bodin, Knolles VI.6 p. 755, recast measuring Bodin and Kepler by q.61 a.2. Honesty: the principle, the books, and Peter’s authorship.
